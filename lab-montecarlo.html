Monte Carlo Convergence Lab

Every Monte Carlo method — pricing an option, simulating neutron transport, integrating a function nobody can solve in closed form — rests on one law: throw enough random samples at a problem and the average converges to the truth, with error shrinking as 1/√N. This lab makes that law visible instead of asserted: watch π emerge from random points, and watch the error track its predicted curve exactly.

Each point is uniform-random in the unit square. Green = inside the quarter circle (x²+y²≤1), gray = outside. π ≈ 4 × (green ÷ total) because the quarter circle's area is exactly π/4 of the square's.

Measured error (dots) vs. sample count, log-log. The line is the predicted error, SE(N) = 4√(p(1-p)/N) — not fit to the data, just the formula. Watch the dots hug the line.

How to read it

The scatter canvas is the method itself: a point lands uniformly in the unit square, and it's colored green if it also falls inside the quarter circle. Over many points, the fraction that land inside approximates the ratio of the two areas — circle-area ÷ square-area = (π/4) ÷ 1 — so multiplying that fraction by 4 estimates π. Nothing about π was assumed going in; it falls out of counting random points.

The error plot is the more important one for HPC work. Both axes are logarithmic, and the predicted-error line has slope -1/2 — that slope is the 1/√N law. Read it as a budget: to cut your error in half, you don't need 2× the samples, you need 4×. To gain one more decimal digit of accuracy (10× smaller error), you need 100× the samples. This is exactly why Monte Carlo methods are simple to write but expensive to run precisely, and exactly why they're such a natural fit for a GPU: the work is embarrassingly parallel (every sample is independent — no communication between them), so throwing more threads at the problem directly buys more decimal places.

Variance reduction (stratified sampling, importance sampling, quasi-random / Sobol sequences) exists specifically to beat the plain 1/√N rate without changing the sample count — a preview of what Track B's Monte Carlo lessons cover after this.
