Probability & Statistics — randomness as a tool, noise as a fact

GPUs meet randomness twice: as a tool (Monte Carlo simulation, dropout, weight init, path tracing) and as a fact of life (your kernel's timings jitter; your benchmark lies without statistics). This lesson covers exactly the probability a GPU professional uses weekly.

Concept

Probability: the fraction of worlds

P(event) is a number in [0, 1]: across all the ways things could go, the fraction where the event happens. Fair die: P(roll 4) = 1/6. The three rules that generate everything you'll need:

complement:   P(not A)   = 1 − P(A)
independent:  P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B)      // only when A tells you nothing about B!
either:       P(A or B)  = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

The multiplication rule is a compounding machine. A kernel that succeeds with P = 0.999 per launch, launched a million times a day: P(zero failures) = 0.999^1,000,000 ≈ e^(10⁶·ln 0.999) ≈ e^−1000 ≈ 0 (Lesson 3's logs turning products into sums). "Rare" times "many" equals "certain" — the mathematics of why flaky code always fails in production.

Random variables, expectation, variance

A random variable X is a number that depends on chance (a die roll, a kernel's runtime). Its expectation E[X] = Σ xᵢ·P(xᵢ) is the long-run average — the fair price of the gamble. Its variance Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] measures spread (in squared units); the square root σ (standard deviation) restores the original units:

die: E[X] = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5
     Var  = E[X²] − μ² = 91/6 − 12.25 ≈ 2.92     σ ≈ 1.71
useful identities:  E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b        Var(aX + b) = a²·Var(X)
independent X, Y:   E[X+Y] = E[X]+E[Y]           Var(X+Y) = Var(X)+Var(Y)

That last line hides the most important √ in engineering: variances of independent things add, so the σ of a sum of N terms grows like √N — while the sum itself grows like N. Noise grows slower than signal. Averages therefore sharpen: the mean of N samples has σ/√N of the individual spread. Everything from "why more benchmark runs help" to "why Monte Carlo error is 1/√N" is this one fact.

Distributions: uniform and normal cover 90% of your needs

Uniform: every value in a range equally likely — curand_uniform(), rand()/RAND_MAX, the raw material all other randomness is cooked from. Normal (Gaussian): the bell curve — arises whenever many small independent effects add up (the Central Limit Theorem), which is why measurement noise, initialization schemes, and aggregate timings all look bell-ish. Parameters: mean μ (center), σ (width). The 68/95/99.7 rule prices rarity: a sample 3σ from the mean happens ~0.3% of the time; at 5σ, ~1 in 3.5 million — "impossible" in tests, several times a day at datacenter scale.

Statistics: telling the truth about noisy measurements

The mean uses all data but follows outliers; the median (middle value when sorted) ignores magnitude of extremes. Kernel timing distributions are right-skewed — there's a floor (the kernel's true cost) and a long tail (JIT compilation, clock ramp-up, another process, thermal throttle) — so the pro convention is:

1. warm up: run the kernel a few times, discard those timings (JIT, caches, clocks)
2. run N ≥ 20 times (100+ for microbenchmarks)
3. report MEDIAN as the headline; report spread (IQR or σ) alongside
4. investigate — don't delete — repeatable outliers: they're a finding, not dirt

Worked example

Task: Estimate π by Monte Carlo, size the error, and decide how many samples a GPU needs for 6-digit accuracy.

Setup: throw N uniform points in a square enclosing a circle. P(inside) = area ratio = π/4. So π ≈ 4·(hits/N). With the diagram's 24 darts: 4·19/24 ≈ 3.17 — right neighborhood, terrible precision.

Error math (the useful part): each dart is a yes/no with p = π/4 ≈ 0.785; variance of one dart = p(1−p) ≈ 0.169. The estimate averages N darts → σ_est = 4·√(0.169/N) ≈ 1.64/√N. Demand 6 digits (σ ≈ 5×10⁻⁷): N ≈ (1.64/5×10⁻⁷)² ≈ 10¹³ samples.

GPU verdict: 10¹³ independent dart throws at ~10¹¹ simple ops/sec effective → ~2 minutes on one GPU — feasible, embarrassingly parallel, and each extra digit costs 100× more work (1/√N strikes: 10× accuracy = 100× samples). Decision made: Monte Carlo buys dimension-independence and trivial parallelism, and pays with that brutal square root. Use it when the problem is high-dimensional or irregular (option pricing, path tracing, physics); use deterministic quadrature when it's smooth and low-dimensional. Knowing which regime you're in is the actual skill.

Key points

  • P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) only under independence; compounding turns rare×many into certain — the math of flaky systems.
  • E[X] is the long-run average; Var(X) = E[(X−μ)²]; for independent variables variances add, so noise of a sum grows √N while signal grows N.
  • The sample mean has spread σ/√N: more runs sharpen estimates, but each accuracy digit costs 100× the samples (Monte Carlo's 1/√N law).
  • Uniform is the raw randomness; normal emerges when many small independent effects add (CLT); 68/95/99.7 within 1/2/3σ prices rarity.
  • Kernel timings are right-skewed: warm up first, run ≥20×, report the median with a spread measure; investigate repeatable outliers.
  • At datacenter scale, 5σ events are daily events — design for the tail, not the mean.

Quiz

1. Two GPUs each fail on a given day with P = 0.001, independently. What's the probability your 2-GPU training job survives 30 days with neither failing?
Survival = 0.999 per GPU-day; 2 GPUs × 30 days = 60 independent survivals: 0.999⁶⁰ = e^(60·ln0.999) ≈ e^−0.06 ≈ 0.9418. (Option A is the linear approximation — close here because P is small, but it's an approximation that fails as probabilities grow, and knowing WHICH is exact matters.) Checkpointing exists because this number decays with every added GPU and day.
2. Your benchmark of a kernel returns (ms): 2.1, 2.0, 2.1, 2.2, 14.9. The right way to report performance?
Median 2.1 resists the outlier and represents typical latency; the 14.9 is data ABOUT your system (first-call JIT? clock ramp? contention?) — investigate, don't hide. Min is defensible for "best case" microbenchmarks but overpromises for production; silent deletion is how benchmark fraud starts.

Exercises

Exercise 1 — A ray tracer shoots 64 sample rays per pixel and averages them. The per-ray brightness estimate has σ = 0.4 (image scale 0..1). Compute the per-pixel noise, the samples needed to halve it, and explain why denoisers beat brute force.

Per-pixel σ = 0.4/√64 = 0.05 — visible speckle. Halving to 0.025 needs 4× the rays (256/pixel) — 1/√N again: each halving quadruples cost. That's why production path tracers stopped chasing N years ago: they render at 1–64 samples and hand the speckle to an ML denoiser (which exploits spatial correlation between neighboring pixels — information brute-force sampling ignores). Trade: bias (denoiser can smear) for a ~100× sample budget cut. Monte Carlo's economics created an entire ML product category.

Exercise 2 — You A/B test a kernel optimization: old median 2.10 ms (σ = 0.06, N = 50), new median 2.04 ms (σ = 0.06, N = 50). Is the 3% win real? Compute the standard error of each estimate and reason it out.

Standard error of each mean ≈ σ/√N = 0.06/√50 ≈ 0.0085 ms. The difference (0.06 ms) is ~7 standard errors of a single estimate (and ~5 of the difference, whose SE = √(0.0085²+0.0085²) ≈ 0.012) — far beyond noise: real win. Contrast: with N = 4 runs each, SE ≈ 0.03, difference = 2 SE — plausibly noise, don't ship the claim. The habit: never announce a perf win without (spread / √N) sanity math; sub-5% "improvements" from n=3 runs are how teams ship regressions with confetti.

Go deeper — the complete guide

Where randomness on a deterministic machine comes from, GPU RNG done right, the CLT and why everything is Gaussian-ish, initialization variance (the σ that makes deep nets trainable), tail thinking, and honest benchmarking end-to-end.

1. Pseudo-randomness: deterministic chaos with a seed

Computers can't roll dice; they iterate functions whose outputs look independent: x_{n+1} = f(x_n). The seed picks the starting point — same seed, same sequence, forever. This is a feature: reproducible experiments, debuggable "randomness," regression tests for stochastic code. The catches: naive generators (old rand(), LCGs) have correlations that ruin simulations; parallel use requires care (next section); and rand() % 6 is subtly biased (RAND_MAX+1 rarely divides by 6 — the low values are slightly more likely; use rejection or floating division). Modern defaults: PCG or Philox on CPU, Philox/XORWOW via cuRAND on GPU; Philox is counter-based — random(key, counter) with no state to carry — which is precisely what 100k threads need.

2. Randomness across 100,000 threads

// WRONG: every thread seeds with time(NULL) → thousands of IDENTICAL streams
// WRONG: seed + tid as seed for an LCG → correlated streams
// RIGHT (cuRAND pattern): one seed, per-thread SUBSEQUENCE:
curand_init(seed, /*subsequence=*/ tid, /*offset=*/ 0, &state);
// RIGHT (Philox counter-based): value = philox(key=seed, ctr={tid, step})

The failure mode is quiet: correlated streams make Monte Carlo converge confidently to the wrong answer — variance looks small because everyone agrees, and everyone is wrong together. Rule: one seed per experiment (logged!), independent subsequences per thread, and a determinism note in your README: parallel reduction order still makes bitwise reproducibility a separate battle (Lesson 8's non-associativity).

3. The Central Limit Theorem, operationally

Sum (or average) many independent, similar-scale effects and the distribution of that sum approaches a Gaussian — regardless of each effect's own shape. Add 12 uniform randoms, subtract 6: a decent standard normal (an old-school trick). Operationally: (a) aggregate timings over many kernels/requests → Gaussian-ish, mean±σ meaningful; (b) per-kernel latency = floor + occasional spikes → NOT Gaussian (skewed), median territory; (c) sums of gradients over a batch → Gaussian-ish, which is why batch-gradient noise is modeled as Gaussian in optimization papers. Knowing when CLT applies is knowing which statistics to trust.

4. Initialization: the variance calculation that unlocked deep learning

A layer computes y = Σᵢ wᵢxᵢ over n inputs (a dot product — everything returns to Lesson 5). If wᵢ are independent with variance v and xᵢ have variance 1, then Var(y) = n·v (variances add). Stack 50 layers: signal variance multiplies by (n·v) each layer → explodes if n·v > 1, dies to zero if < 1. The fix is school algebra: choose v = 1/n (Xavier; or 2/n for ReLU — Kaiming/He — because ReLU zeroes half the variance):

W = torch.randn(n_out, n_in) * math.sqrt(2.0 / n_in)   # He init, in the flesh

That one line — "pick σ so variances neither explode nor vanish through depth" — is arguably the most consequential application of Var(aX) = a²Var(X) in history: without it, pre-2010s deep nets simply didn't train. When you see std=0.02 in a GPT config, that's this calculation tuned for its layer widths (and there's a matching ×1/√d inside attention — same theorem, same reason).

5. Expectation as a design tool: dropout and stochastic estimates

Dropout zeroes each activation with probability p at train time. To keep the expected activation unchanged, survivors are scaled by 1/(1−p): E[output] = (1−p)·x/(1−p) + p·0 = x. This "unbiased estimator" pattern — inject randomness, correct in expectation — recurs across GPU computing: stochastic rounding (Lesson 8's cousin: round up with probability equal to the fraction, making rounding error mean-zero), Monte Carlo gradient estimators, randomized sketches for huge matrix problems, even GPU texture sampling with jitter. The discipline is always the same two lines: what's the expectation? what's the variance you paid?

6. Tails, percentiles, and the SLO mindset

Serving systems report P50/P95/P99 latency, not means: users experience the tail (one slow token stalls the whole streamed reply). Facts to carry: percentiles don't add (P99 of a two-stage pipeline ≠ sum of stage P99s — you must measure end-to-end or convolve); at 10⁶ requests/day, P99.9 events happen 1000 times a day; and a Gaussian assumption under-prices tails badly whenever effects multiply rather than add (skewed/log-normal-ish reality). GPU-specific tail sources you'll hunt in Track A10: JIT/compilation on first shape, clock/power state transitions, memory fragmentation, preemption by other tenants, and stragglers in multi-GPU collectives (NCCL waits for the slowest — max of N random variables, which has its own distribution and grows with N).

7. Gotchas

Assuming independence. Two "independent" failures sharing a power supply, thermal zone, or driver aren't. Multiplying P's without independence is how "impossible" outages get scheduled.
Benchmarking without warmup. First iterations pay JIT (torch.compile!), cache fills, and clock ramp — folding them into the mean fabricates regressions. Discard, or report "cold start" as its own (valuable) number.
Same seed everywhere. Thousands of threads or data loaders seeded identically → duplicated "random" work, correlated Monte Carlo, and augmentations that repeat across workers (a real PyTorch DataLoader pitfall — seed per worker).
σ² vs σ. Variance is in squared units; reporting Var where σ is expected inflates spread quadratically. And sample variance divides by N−1 (Bessel), not N — small-N spreads are otherwise underestimated.
rand() % n. Modulo bias whenever n doesn't divide the generator's range; fine for a game drop table, fatal for a simulation. Use the library's ranged API or rejection sampling.
Deleting outliers. A repeatable 15 ms spike every ~64 calls is a finding (allocator? cache set conflict? clock governor?), not noise. Median it out of the headline, then chase it — that's where real perf work hides.

Scenario — the "flaky" benchmark that was three real bugs

You inherit a CI perf gate that teams ignore because "it's flaky — fails 1 run in 10 with a 30% regression that vanishes on retry." Statistics audit, using only this lesson. (1) The gate compares single runs: old vs new, one sample each. With per-run σ ≈ 8%, a 2-sample comparison flags phantom 20% regressions constantly (difference of two noisy numbers has σ√2 ≈ 11%; a 2σ event per comparison × dozens of comparisons per day = daily false alarms — rare×many). Fix: 20 warm runs each side, compare medians, alert at 5× the standard error. (2) The remaining repeatable spikes correlate with the first run after model reload — JIT compilation counted as regression. Fix: warmup pass, plus a separate tracked "cold start" metric (it matters for serving!). (3) One kernel's timings are bimodal — 2.1 ms or 3.4 ms, nothing between: two clock states; the box's power governor was "balanced." Fix: lock clocks for benchmarks (nvidia-smi -lgc), document that prod won't have locked clocks — report both. Result: gate goes from ignored to trusted, catches a real 4% regression the next month (5 standard errors — no argument possible). Nothing here needed calculus: multiplication rule, σ/√N, medians, and the refusal to delete outliers.

Practice

1. Fair coin flipped 10 times: P(at least one head)? An H100 with P(uncorrectable ECC error) = 10⁻⁵ per day, fleet of 10 000: expected errors/day, and P(zero errors) — compute both.
P(≥1 head) = 1 − (1/2)¹⁰ = 1023/1024 ≈ 0.999. Fleet: expected = 10⁴·10⁻⁵ = 0.1 errors/day. P(zero) = (1−10⁻⁵)^10⁴ ≈ e^−0.1 ≈ 0.905 — one day in ten, somewhere, a job needs its checkpoint. (Expected value adds even when events aren't independent; the survival product needs independence.)
2. X uniform on [0, 1]: E[X] = 1/2. Compute Var(X) = E[X²] − μ² given E[X²] = 1/3, then σ of the average of 1200 such samples.
Var = 1/3 − 1/4 = 1/12 ≈ 0.0833; σ = 0.289. Average of 1200: σ/√1200 ≈ 0.289/34.6 ≈ 0.0083. (The 1/12 is worth recognizing: it's the variance behind "add 12 uniforms" ≈ normal with σ = 1.)
3. Timings (ms): 5.1, 5.0, 5.2, 5.1, 5.0, 9.7, 5.1, 5.2. Give mean, median, which to report, and TWO distinct hypotheses for the 9.7 with the test you'd run for each.
Mean = 5.68, median = 5.1. Report median 5.1 (±0.08-ish IQR). Hypotheses: (a) warmup/JIT — test: is the spike always the first iteration? re-run with explicit warmup; (b) periodic interference (another process, clock transition, allocator) — test: longer series, look for periodicity/correlation with position, lock clocks and isolate the GPU. If it's reproducibly positional, it's structure, not noise.
4. Prove the dropout scaling: activation a, dropout probability p = 0.2, survivors scaled by 1/0.8. Show E[output] = a and compute Var(output/a) — what did the variance cost?
Output = a/0.8 with P = 0.8, else 0. E = 0.8·a/0.8 = a ✓. E[out²] = 0.8·a²/0.64 = 1.25a² → Var = 1.25a² − a² = 0.25a², so relative σ = 0.5: unbiased in expectation, but you injected 50% relative noise per unit — the regularization IS the noise. Expectation-correct, variance-paid: the pattern's full price tag, visible.
5. A Monte Carlo light-transport estimate has σ = 2.0 at N = 1024 samples. Samples for σ = 0.1? If importance sampling reduces per-sample σ by 5×, samples then? What's the general lesson?
σ ∝ 1/√N: need (2.0/0.1)² = 400× → N ≈ 409 600. With 5× lower per-sample σ (0.4 base): (0.4/0.1)² = 16× → N ≈ 16 384 — 25× cheaper. Lesson: variance REDUCTION (importance sampling, stratification, control variates) beats sample brute force, because per-sample σ enters squared while N enters linearly. This is why every serious Monte Carlo system is a variance-reduction system.
6. Your service's per-request latency is roughly log-normal. The team sets an alert on mean latency. Name two concrete ways this alert lies, and what to monitor instead.
(1) Skew: mean sits well above median — alert fires on harmless tail wiggle or, tuned looser, misses median regressions users feel. (2) A few extreme outliers move the mean while P50/P95 are stable → pages for non-events (and the reverse: P99 doubling while the mean barely moves → silent user pain). Monitor percentiles (P50/P95/P99) end-to-end, alert on the ones tied to SLOs, keep an outlier-count metric separately.
7. Design: a GPU particle simulation needs 10⁷ independent random directions per frame, reproducible across runs and across GPU models, with no thread reading another's state. Specify generator choice, seeding scheme, uniform-direction method, and the reproducibility caveats you'd document.
Generator: counter-based Philox — value = f(key, counter), stateless per thread. Seeding: key = experiment_seed (logged); counter = (frame_id, particle_id, draw_index) — unique per draw, no collisions, no stored state. Uniform direction in 3D: draw z ~ U[−1,1], φ ~ U[0,2π), dir = (√(1−z²)cosφ, √(1−z²)sinφ, z) — Lesson 4's circle math; (naive normalize-a-cube-sample biases toward corners; rejection sampling wastes ~48% draws and breaks the fixed counter budget — reasons to prefer the direct method). Caveats to document: bitwise identical across runs on same binary/GPU ✓; across GPU models, floating-point contraction (FMA) and libm differences may flip last bits — pin math flags (-fmad=false for strictness) or promise statistical, not bitwise, reproducibility. (That caveat is Lesson 8, arriving next.)
