How to read it

Amdahl's law is one line: overall speedup = 1 ÷ ( (1−p) + p⁄s ), where p is the fraction of the work that's parallelizable and s is how much faster you make that parallel part. The magic — and the trap — is in the limit. Push s to infinity (an infinitely fast GPU) and the p/s term vanishes, leaving ceiling = 1 ÷ (1−p). That ceiling depends only on the serial fraction: 10% serial caps you at 10×, 1% serial at 100×, no matter how much hardware you buy.

The practical lessons are liberating, not depressing. First, a blazing-fast kernel is not enough: if 20% of your program is serial, you're capped at 5× overall even with a perfect GPU — so the left canvas's yellow slice is what to attack. Second, this is why large-scale computing obsesses over driving the serial fraction toward zero (overlapping communication, parallelizing setup, avoiding global synchronization): halving the serial part doubles your ceiling. Third, it explains the classic "we put it on the GPU and only got 3×" disappointment — measure the whole pipeline, not just the kernel, and find the serial part first. This is the honest companion to the Roofline: the roofline tells you how fast one kernel can go; Amdahl tells you how much that kernel can help the program.

Note the optimistic counterpart, Gustafson's law (Lesson 0.4): in practice people don't re-run a fixed job faster — given a bigger machine they solve a bigger problem, which grows the parallel fraction toward 100% and restores near-linear scaling. Amdahl bounds a fixed problem; Gustafson describes scaling the problem to the machine. Both are true.
