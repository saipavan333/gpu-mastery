Reduction & Scan Animator

Summing an array looks inherently sequential — add them up one at a time. On a GPU you instead fold the array in a tree, halving the survivors each step, so N numbers collapse to one answer in only log₂N steps. The same tree idea, run in reverse, gives you a prefix scan — a running total at every position — which powers sorting, stream compaction, and sparse math. Step through both and watch the depth stay logarithmic while the width shrinks.

How to read it

Each row is the array after one parallel step; arrows show which additions happen. In the reduction, step one pairs the two halves — thread i adds element i + N/2 into element i — then the stride halves and halves again until a single sum survives in slot 0. Because the number of live elements halves every step, the depth is log₂N: a 1024-element sum finishes in 10 steps instead of 1023. This is the addressing pattern from Lesson 5.8, and it is deliberately the sequential-addressing version, where active threads stay packed on the left — that keeps a warp's live lanes contiguous and avoids the divergence that the naive interleaved pattern suffers.

The prefix scan shown here is Hillis-Steele: at step with distance d = 1, 2, 4, …, every element adds the element d positions to its left, and after log₂N steps each slot holds the running total up to and including itself. It is beautifully shallow — log₂N depth — but notice the work counter: it performs about N·log₂N additions, more than the N−1 a sequential loop would do. That is the classic trade in parallel algorithms: step efficiency versus work efficiency. The work-efficient alternative, Blelloch scan, does only O(N) work with an up-sweep and a down-sweep over the tree — at N = 16 that is 30 adds versus Hillis-Steele's 49, and the gap widens with N. Reductions are already work-optimal at N−1 adds; scans force you to choose, and the right choice depends on whether you are short on cores or short on memory bandwidth.

Both patterns are the backbone of GPU computing: reductions sum losses, norms, and dot products; scans allocate output slots in parallel, drive radix sort, and build the index arrays behind sparse matrices (Track B) and rendering (Track C). Master the tree and a surprising amount of "inherently sequential" work turns out to be log₂N deep.
