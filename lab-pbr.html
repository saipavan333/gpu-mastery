PBR Material Explorer

Every physically based renderer — game engine, offline movie renderer, this course's Track C capstone — shades a surface with the same three-term Cook-Torrance BRDF: a distribution term, a geometry term, and a Fresnel term. This lab computes that exact math, per pixel, live in your browser (no WebGL, just canvas pixels), so you can watch what each parameter actually does instead of taking the formula on faith.

How to read it

Each term answers a different physical question about the surface at the point you're looking at:

D — the normal distribution function (GGX/Trowbridge-Reitz). A rough surface is really billions of tiny mirror facets pointing in slightly different directions. D is the fraction of those facets pointing exactly halfway between the light and your eye — the only orientation that can bounce light from the light source straight to you. Low roughness means most facets agree on one direction, so D spikes into a tight, bright highlight; high roughness spreads facets over many directions, so D is low everywhere but nonzero over a wide area — a soft, broad highlight. Try dragging roughness from 1 down to 0.045 and watch the highlight go from a soft glow to a tiny hard point.

G — geometric shadowing/masking (Smith). Even a facet pointed the right way can be blocked: shadowed from the light by a neighboring facet, or masked from your eye by one in front of it. This effect gets worse at grazing angles and on rougher surfaces (taller, more occluding facets), so G quietly dims the response as the surface tilts away from you or the light — without it, rough surfaces would look unrealistically bright at grazing angles.

F — Fresnel (Schlick's approximation). Every material, metal or not, reflects almost all light at a glancing enough angle — it's why still water looks like a mirror at a shallow viewing angle but transparent looking straight down. F0 (reflectance straight-on) is what actually differs: about 0.02–0.05 for non-metals regardless of color, and the material's own color for metals. That's the entire metallic/non-metallic split in one number — drag metallic from 0 to 1 on a colored preset and watch the tint move from "colored diffuse surface with a faint white highlight" to "colorless diffuse gone, color now lives entirely in the reflection."

Honest limitation, not a bug: at very low roughness with only a single point light and no environment reflections, a "mirror" material looks almost black except for one tiny bright dot — because a perfect mirror only ever reflects the one direction the light is actually in. Real renders add image-based lighting (a full environment to reflect) precisely so smooth metals look shiny everywhere, not just at the highlight; that's beyond this lab's single-light scope, but now you know why a chrome ball under one lamp looks mostly dark in a plain photo too.
