torch.compile — your kernel skills, automated; your job, moved up a level

torch.compile captures your Python model into graphs, fuses the glue, and emits Triton — literally A4's language, written by a machine applying A5's ledger. This lesson teaches you to operate that machine: what breaks its capture, when its wins are real, why it recompiles, and how to read the kernels it writes — because "the compiler did something slow" is only debuggable by someone who knows what it should have done.

Concept

The stack: capture → differentiate → generate

model_c = torch.compile(model)          # first call: seconds of compilation
Dynamo      hooks CPython frame evaluation, symbolically traces your Python
            into an FX graph, and installs GUARDS (asserts on shapes/dtypes/
            constants) that decide when the cached graph is still valid
AOTAutograd traces the JOINT forward+backward (A2's tape, ahead of time —
            so the BACKWARD gets fused too: eager autograd never could)
Inductor    plans fusion groups (A5 §1's boundary rules, encoded), picks
            tile sizes, emits Triton for GPU / C++ for CPU — A4's kernels,
            generated: TORCH_LOGS=output_code shows you the literal source
modes: default | reduce-overhead (wraps steps in CUDA GRAPHS — 5.9 §2's
  one-launch replay, killing 5.2's per-launch floor for confetti models)
  | max-autotune (A4's autotuner, given a bigger budget)

Graph breaks: where capture surrenders

break causes (the usual suspects, all old friends):
  .item() / .cpu() / print(tensor)   → needs a device VALUE in Python:
      5.3's sync trap AND a capture wall — one line, two sins (A2 gotcha 2)
  data-dependent control flow        → `if loss < 0.1:` on a tensor — the
      graph can't branch on values it hasn't computed (use torch.where, or
      torch.cond for real cases)
  unsupported calls                  → numpy ops mid-forward, arbitrary C
      extensions WITHOUT a fake kernel (A3 §2's registration debt, collected)
audit tool: torch._dynamo.explain(fn)(x) — lists every break WITH its reason
  and line number. The 5.10 discipline applies: count breaks BEFORE tuning
  anything; one break in a hot inner loop nullifies the whole exercise.

Guards and recompilation: the cache you didn't know you had

each compiled graph is cached under its GUARDS: input shapes, dtypes,
requires_grad, closure constants, even dict key order. A guard miss →
RECOMPILE (seconds, on the critical path — A4 quiz 2's autotune stall,
one level up). Shape churn is the classic trigger:
  dynamic=True / torch._dynamo.mark_dynamic(x, 0)   → compile with SYMBOLIC
  batch dim: one graph serves many sizes (guards become inequalities)
  torch._dynamo.config.cache_size_limit (default ~8/frame): exceed it and
  dynamo quietly FALLS BACK TO EAGER — your speedup evaporates silently
  (gotcha 4's monitoring hook lives here)
production stance: compile is a stateful JIT SERVICE inside your process —
  warm it deliberately, monitor recompiles like you monitor 5.4's errors.

When it wins (the table you already derived)

wins:  many small ops (norms/activations/optimizers — A5 §1's ledger, auto-
       collapsed), launch-bound confetti (reduce-overhead's CUDA graphs
       erase 5.2's floor), long steady-shape runs (amortization)
draws: matmul-dominated models (cuBLAS was already at the roofline — 5.11
       quiz 1; compile fuses the EPILOGUES and stops there)
loses: shape-churning notebooks, break-riddled code, cold-start-sensitive
       serving without warmup (quiz 2's whole story)

Worked example

Task: Compile a transformer MLP block (linear → bias → GELU → linear → bias → residual → layernorm) and audit what the compiler did — the A5 ledger, executed by machine. (Colab-runnable; representative timings from a T4 run.)

eager:    ~11 kernel launches/step (profiler count: 2 GEMMs + 9 glue kernels)
compiled: 4 kernels — 2 cuBLAS GEMMs (untouched — the compiler KNOWS 5.11
          quiz 1) + 2 generated Triton kernels (bias+GELU fused into one;
          residual+layernorm's two-pass into another)
timing (do_bench, warmed):  glue portion 0.71 ms → 0.24 ms  (the A5 §1
          ledger's ÷3, delivered automatically); GEMMs unchanged; step
          1.9× on this glue-heavy block, ~1.15× on a GEMM-dominated one
          (a9-when's two rows, measured in one afternoon)
the audit trail you should actually read:
  torch._dynamo.explain(block)(x)   → "0 graph breaks" (clean capture)
  TORCH_LOGS=output_code            → the generated Triton: tl.load with
      masks, BLOCK constexprs, pointer arithmetic in elements — A4's
      vocabulary verbatim; find the fused gelu formula inline in the loop
      body and the fp32 accumulation on the layernorm stats (A6's policy,
      encoded by the compiler because its authors read the same postmortems)

What to internalize: the compiler applied YOUR playbook — fuse the glue (A5), leave cuBLAS alone (5.11), accumulate stats wide (A6), mask the tails (A4) — and it will apply that playbook tirelessly to every model you ever compile. Your value moved up a level: you now audit its output (read the Triton!), clear its obstacles (kill the breaks), feed it stable shapes, and hand-write only what it structurally cannot do — fusing INTO the GEMM itself, which is exactly A11's capstone.

Key points

  • The stack is capture (Dynamo + guards) → joint fwd/bwd tracing (AOTAutograd) → fusion and Triton codegen (Inductor): your A4/A5 skills, mechanized — and auditable via explain() and TORCH_LOGS=output_code.
  • Graph breaks (.item(), data-dependent Python, unregistered custom ops) split the model into eager-stitched islands: count them FIRST — one break in the hot loop nullifies the tuning that follows.
  • Guards cache compiled graphs by shape/dtype/constants; misses recompile ON the critical path, and exceeding cache_size_limit silently falls back to eager — shape stability (mark_dynamic, bucketing) is a production requirement, not a nicety.
  • reduce-overhead mode wraps steps in CUDA graphs (5.9 §2): the cure for launch-bound confetti (5.2's floor), with static-address and no-sync constraints that your 5.3/5.9 discipline already satisfies.
  • Wins scale with glue-share (A5's ledger) and run length (amortization): compiled GEMM-dominated models improve ~1.1×, glue-heavy blocks ~2× — predict from the profile before promising numbers (a9-when).
  • Compile is a stateful JIT service in prod: warm every (shape, dtype) key at startup, export recompile/fallback counters, and treat a recompile storm like a 5.4 error class — monitored, alarmed, root-caused.

Quiz

1. After enabling torch.compile in a serving worker, p50 improves 25% — but every ~40 minutes a request stalls 6–9 seconds, and the stalls correlate with unusual request sizes. Meanwhile a counter you wisely exported shows compiled-graph count growing without bound. Diagnose the mechanism, the eventual silent failure it's marching toward, and the two-layer fix.
The graph-count counter is the hero: recompile pathologies are invisible in averages and fatal in tails, and the SILENT eager-fallback ending is the part almost nobody knows until it bites (no exception is thrown — by design). The bucketing fix is the same shape-stabilization you met in A4 quiz 2 and A8's serving schedulers — three systems, one law: lazily-specializing machinery converts shape entropy into critical-path latency, so you spend entropy down BEFORE the hot path.
2. A colleague benchmarks torch.compile on their research notebook: "2.4 s eager vs 31 s compiled — compile is garbage." Their loop: fresh model per cell, 5 iterations, different batch size each iteration, one .item() print per step. List everything wrong with the measurement AND the honest prediction for their model if measured correctly (it's a small GNN: mostly scatter/gather ops with tiny dense layers).
The three sins map to this lesson's three mechanisms (amortization, guards, breaks) — a tidy diagnostic checklist for every "compile made it slower" report you'll ever receive (you will receive many). The GNN prediction is the deeper skill: the compiler automates A5's ledger but cannot repeal 5.7's physics — fusion helps glue, not gathers. max-autotune (option 3) would spend MORE compile time tuning kernels that were never the problem: a knob turned without a diagnosis (5.10's cardinal sin).

Then recompile with mode='max-autotune' and diff: what changed, what didn't, and what did it cost?

(b) tl.float32 casts around the mean/var reduction on bf16 inputs;
(c) extern_kernels.mm / addmm lines — the compiler DELEGATING to cuBLAS.

Reading machine-generated kernels without flinching is the graduation exercise of A4+A9: from here on, "what did the compiler do" is a question you answer with evidence in five minutes, which is precisely the skill A11's r1-vs-r2 bar requires.

Go deeper — operating the compiler

1. CUDA graphs via reduce-overhead (5.9 §2, productized)

mode="reduce-overhead": after warmup, the whole step's kernel sequence is
captured ONCE and replayed with a single launch — 5.2's per-kernel µs floor
amortized to ~zero: transformative for small-model/small-batch serving
(decode loops! A8's per-token confetti) where launch overhead rivaled math.
constraints (all 5.3/5.9 alumni): static tensor ADDRESSES between replays
(the runtime maintains a static input pool — feed via copy-in), no
data-dependent shapes inside, no syncs/.item() (breaks capture anyway), RNG
handled specially. When it works: p50 drops like quiz 1's 25%. When violated:
cryptic replay errors or silent recapture churn — the 5.9 scenario's lint
rule (justify every sync) is literally a cudagraph-readiness audit.

2. AOTInductor & the deployment boundary (A10's door)

torch.compile is a JIT living in your Python process (guards, cache, warmup).
AOTInductor EXPORTS the compiled artifact ahead of time (.so + weights):
no Python, no Dynamo, no first-request compile — the serving-friendly shape
(and why A10's images must otherwise carry the whole Triton/C++ toolchain:
a JIT compiles IN PRODUCTION, so prod needs the compiler — the container
gotcha people discover at 2 a.m.). The mental model: compile-the-JIT for
training/research velocity; export-the-artifact for fleets — the same
JIT-vs-AOT trade as 5.1's fatbin PTX story, one abstraction up.

3. Gotchas

Timing the compiler. First calls include capture+codegen+autotune (quiz 2's 31 s). Warmup until steady, then measure — and report compile time SEPARATELY (it's a real cost, just a different budget line).
Silent eager fallback. cache_size_limit exceeded, or an internal error → dynamo runs eager without raising. Export and alarm on the counters (torch._dynamo.utils.counters) or your "compiled" fleet quietly isn't.
Side effects reordered or duplicated. Python side effects in forward (appending to lists, logging, RNG calls outside torch) happen at TRACE time, not run time (A4 gotcha 2's law, model-scale). Keep forward pure; hooks and callbacks go outside the compiled region.
Global flags changing under a cached graph. tf32 settings, deterministic mode, autocast policy — flipped AFTER compilation, some are guarded (recompile), some are baked (stale behavior). Pin numerics-affecting globals before compile and treat them as part of the model version (A6 P3's CI heisenbug, compiled edition).
Compile as a rescue for bad kernels. It cannot fix your custom op's uncoalesced access (5.7 is physics), un-break an unregistered extension (A3 §2's debt), or re-algorithm an O(N²) attention (A5 exists for that). The compiler optimizes the graph BETWEEN your mistakes.
Version-skew fragility. Compiled-artifact caches and generated code are torch-version-coupled; upgrading torch under a warm cache directory or shipping artifacts across versions invites subtle breakage — pin, rebuild, re-fixture per upgrade (A3 §2's stable-ABI note is about the C++ side; the compiler side still moves).

Scenario — the 30% win that came with a 40-minute time bomb

A serving team enables reduce-overhead compile on their embedding+ranker model: p50 −30%, dashboards celebrated, change frozen for the quarter. Three weeks later: rare 8-second request stalls, then a pager storm one Tuesday — p99 through the roof while p50 stays gorgeous. The on-call, compile-literate, pulls the two counters this lesson said to export: compiled-graph count = 847 and climbing; eager-fallback = just started incrementing. Root cause hunt, guided by guards: a upstream service began sending a rare "batch of 1 with trailing metadata column" shape — every occurrence minted a new guard set (shape × a CLOSURE CONSTANT: a feature-flag dict whose key order varied per pod!), recompiles burned 6–8 s each on the request path, and that Tuesday the cache limit tripped → silent eager fleet-wide → p50 drift that DIDN'T page because it was slow, not broken (gotcha 2's exact trajectory). Fixes, in the order deployed: shape bucketing at the API gateway (entropy spent before the hot path — quiz 1's law), the flag dict frozen into a tuple (guard surface minimized), warmup-per-bucket in the readiness probe, and the two counters promoted to SLO-adjacent alarms. The postmortem's title is the lesson's thesis: "torch.compile is a stateful service we deployed without realizing we'd deployed a service." Treat the JIT like you treat any cache-plus-compiler system — warm it, bound it, monitor it — and it pays its 30% forever; treat it as a flag, and it pays until the first novel Tuesday.

Practice

1. Predict compile's win (big/modest/negative) with one-line mechanisms: (a) BERT-large fine-tune, fixed 512 seq; (b) tiny MLP policy net in an RL loop, batch 1, called 10k×/s; (c) research notebook, new architecture daily; (d) 70B decode serving already on CUDA graphs + FA; (e) a diffusion U-Net with hundreds of convs+norms+activations; (f) your A3 custom-op-heavy model, fake kernels unregistered.
(a) Modest-solid (1.2–1.5×): GEMM-heavy but real glue share; stable shapes = perfect amortization. (b) BIG: launch-bound confetti — reduce-overhead's CUDA graphs erase 5.2's floor (this is the poster child). (c) Negative: daily recompiles, shape churn, compile tax never amortizes (a9-when's losing row — use eager, keep velocity). (d) ~Nothing left: the stack already hand-applied every trick compile knows (it's chasing 5.11's cuBLAS bar from below). (e) BIG: hundreds of fusable norm/act/conv-epilogue seams — the A5 ledger's dream client. (f) NEGATIVE until A3 §2's debt is paid: every unregistered op is a break; islands don't fuse — register fake kernels FIRST, then re-run the ledger. Six predictions, six mechanisms, zero benchmarks needed to rank them — benchmarks then confirm (M1 L7's order).
2. Explain guards to a new teammate via three concrete misses: same code called with (a) batch 32 then 33; (b) fp16 then bf16 input; (c) a module whose config dict gained a key. For each: what was guarded, miss cost, and the stabilization. Then: why does dynamic=True not simply solve all three?
(a) Shape guard (s0==32): miss → recompile; stabilize with mark_dynamic/bucketing — becomes a symbolic-range guard. (b) Dtype guard: recompile is CORRECT here (different kernels are genuinely needed — A6's dispatch reality); stabilize by not flip-flopping dtypes per call (pick the policy). (c) Constant/structure guard on the closure (dict keys!): the scenario's silent assassin — stabilize by freezing config into immutable structures before compile. dynamic=True only generalizes SHAPES (symbolic ints with 0/1 specialization); dtype and structure guards remain exact by necessity — a graph fused for fp16 math cannot serve bf16. The teaching: guards are a CORRECTNESS mechanism (5.4's spirit: refuse silently-wrong reuse) that becomes a performance problem only when your inputs' entropy exceeds your warmup's coverage — entropy management, not guard avoidance, is the fix (quiz 1).
3. reduce-overhead requires static input addresses across replays. Explain why (what does a captured CUDA graph actually store? — 5.9 §2), how torch satisfies it (the static-input copy-in pool = which 5.3/5.9 pattern?), and the failure signature when a user tensor's storage moves anyway.
A captured graph records kernel launches with their ARGUMENT VALUES — device pointers baked in (5.9 §2: record once, replay cheap; the replay re-issues the same pointers). New iteration, new input allocation → the graph would read stale addresses. torch's fix: a static input pool — persistent buffers at fixed addresses; each step COPIES user inputs in, replays, copies out: 5.3 P6's pinned-pool/ring pattern, repurposed (stable addresses as the contract instead of DMA-ability). Failure signature: silent staleness is prevented by design (torch copies), but OUT-of-graph tensors captured accidentally (a Python-held reference to an intermediate) or allocator churn under memory pressure → "tensor used in graph capture was freed/resized" errors or recapture storms — the fix is the 5.9 lint: no allocations, no syncs, no shape changes inside the captured region. One mental model: a CUDA graph is a compiled 5.9 pipeline; treat its inputs like memory-mapped registers, not Python objects.
4. Your compiled training step shows in the profiler as: [big fused kernel][big fused kernel][200 µs GAP][eager island: 14 tiny kernels][big fused kernel]. Reconstruct what the code likely looks like, the two-command investigation, and the fix's expected shape.
The eager island bracketed by fused regions = a graph break mid-step: 14 tiny kernels of un-fused glue running eager, plus the gap (guard evaluation + Python transition costs — the toll EVERY step, 5.2's floor's cousin). Likely code: a metrics/logging line (.item(), tensor print), a data-dependent if, or an unregistered op right between two module groups. Investigation: (1) torch._dynamo.explain → the break's reason + line; (2) profiler's CPU row at the gap (5.10's read-the-whole-timeline law) → see the Python frame names confirming it. Fix shape: move the offending line outside the step (log every N steps from an on-device accumulator — A2 gotcha 2), or torch.where the branch, or register the op's fake kernel (A3 §2) → expected: islands merge, the 14 kernels fuse into ~2, the 200 µs toll disappears per step — at 5k steps/hour that's real money (the arithmetic habit, always).
5. Design the compile rollout playbook for a 40-model inference fleet (the A10 preview): the four pre-prod gates, the two runtime counters with alarm thresholds, the warmup contract, and the rollback trigger — each tied to a mechanism from this lesson.
Gates: (1) explain() break count == 0 (or waivered with owner) per model — breaks are per-model debt (a9-breaks); (2) fixture parity vs eager at deployment dtype (compiled numerics differ in fused order — A5 P6's policy applies); (3) shape-bucket inventory declared + warmup script covering every bucket (quiz 1's entropy budget); (4) cold-start budget measured: compile-or-load(AOTInductor) time within deploy SLO (§2's JIT-vs-AOT decision made explicit per model). Counters: recompile rate (alarm: >0 sustained in steady state — every recompile is an unplanned shape) and eager-fallback count (alarm: >0, PERIOD — the silent win-evaporation, gotcha 2). Warmup contract: readiness probe replays one request per bucket per dtype — pods serve only after (the scenario's fix, standardized). Rollback trigger: p99 regression OR fallback alarm → flag off per model (compile must be a per-model flag, never fleet-global). The playbook is quiz 1 + the scenario, turned into checkboxes — which is what playbooks are for.
6. Compare torch.compile's philosophy to writing everything in A4 Triton by hand, along four axes: coverage, peak, maintenance, and debuggability — ending with the boundary rule for when a hand kernel enters a compiled codebase (A11's admission test).
Coverage: compile touches EVERY op in every model automatically (the fleet's long tail no human would hand-fuse); hand-Triton covers what you write. Peak: hand wins where structure exceeds the compiler's fusion rules (into-GEMM epilogues, cross-op algebraic tricks like flash's rescale — A5); compile matches or beats hand on routine glue (its search never gets bored). Maintenance: compiled code re-optimizes per torch release and new hardware for free; hand kernels rot (5.6's register-weather, A3 P6's arch churn). Debuggability: compile adds a layer (guards/recompiles/generated code) but with tools (explain, LOGS); hand kernels are transparent but YOURS at 3 a.m. Boundary rule: a hand kernel enters when it beats mode='max-autotune' at production shapes with fixtures attached, and it ships with an eager-twin oracle + an owner — otherwise the compiler's version wins by total-cost (A11's r1-vs-r2 bar, stated as policy). This four-axis answer is also precisely quiz-grade interview material.
7. Interview closer: "What actually happens when I wrap my model in torch.compile?" — 90 seconds: the three-stage stack with each stage's artifact, guards and their production consequences, what Inductor's output literally is, the two modes worth naming, the honest win table, and the closing sentence about where the engineer's job moves.
Skeleton: "Three machines run. Dynamo hooks Python's frame evaluation and traces your forward into an FX graph, installing GUARDS — asserts on shapes, dtypes, and captured constants that decide cache validity; a guard miss recompiles on the spot, which is why shape discipline and warmup are production requirements, and why exceeding the cache limit silently reverts to eager — I export those counters [quiz 1, scenario].
